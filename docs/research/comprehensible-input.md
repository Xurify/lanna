# Comprehensible Input and Lanna

_Lanna research note, 28 October 2026. The same note as a page: https://claude.ai/artifact/Tt3n8CesrkeKGfwt1fMErK._

What the comprehensible-input school claims, what forty years of research kept and threw out, where the app stands against it, and what changed in the app as a result.

**Short answer.** Before today the app followed comprehensible input only in two corners: Story kept to about 98% known words, and every lesson sentence is a real sentence with audio. The rest was form-focused drilling (cloze, typed endings, spaced review), which is one of the four things a balanced course needs, but it was taking roughly three quarters of the planned minutes. The research does not say "switch to pure input". For a learner who already reads at B1 but produces A1 endings, pure input is the one method with a documented failure mode. It says: make input a real strand, meet a form in a sentence before you are tested on it, and keep the balance visible. That is what the app now does.

## What comprehensible input is

Stephen Krashen's input hypothesis (1982, 1985) says we acquire a language in one way only: by understanding messages that are a little beyond our current level. He wrote that as _i+1_. Everything else in his model follows from it. Conscious study of rules ("learning") produces knowledge that can only edit output after the fact. Speaking and writing are the result of acquisition, not its cause. Anxiety and pressure raise an "affective filter" that blocks input. A beginner is allowed a silent period.

The methods built on this idea all put a large volume of easy, interesting listening and reading at the centre:

- **Extensive reading.** Many easy books, chosen by the learner, read for pleasure, with almost every word already known (Day and Bamford). The same idea applied to audio is extensive listening.
- **TPRS.** Teaching Proficiency through Reading and Storytelling (Blaine Ray, 1990s): the class builds a story together, the teacher asks dozens of easy questions about it ("circling"), and the story is then read.
- **Dreaming Spanish / ALG.** Hundreds of hours of graded video, no grammar, no flashcards, and advice not to speak until about 600 hours of listening. The roadmap puts a "patient speaker" at 300 hours and a comfortable conversation at 1,000.

## What the evidence says

The field has had forty years to test this. The picture in 2026 is clear enough to build on.

### 1. Input is necessary and central. Nobody disputes that any more.

Lichtman and VanPatten's review "Was Krashen right? Forty years later" (2021) finds that every current theory of second-language acquisition treats input as the main raw material, and that "comprehension precedes production" holds up. They prefer the phrase "communicatively embedded input": sentences that carry a message you care about, not sentences shown as examples of a rule.

### 2. "i+1" cannot be measured, but word coverage can, and it is the usable version.

Nobody has found a way to test whether a text is exactly one step beyond a learner. What has been measured is the share of running words a reader knows. Hu and Nation (2000) put comfortable unassisted reading at about 98% known words. Schmitt, Jiang and Grabe (2011) found no cliff, just a straight line: each extra percent of words known buys about 2.3 points of comprehension.

| Words known | Comprehension (Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe 2011) |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| 90%         | 50%                                         |
| 95%         | 60%                                         |
| 98%         | 68%                                         |
| 100%        | 75%                                         |

661 readers of two academic texts. The relationship is linear in the measured range; even knowing every word gave only 75%, because background knowledge does the rest. Lanna aims its input at 95 to 98% known words: hard enough to carry something new, easy enough to read instead of decode.

For listening the bar is a little lower: van Zeeland and Schmitt (2013) found informal listening comfortable at about 95% coverage, because speech carries tone, rhythm and context that text does not.

### 3. Extensive reading works, and the effect is medium-sized.

Two meta-analyses of classroom extensive-reading programs, Nakanishi (2015, 34 studies) and Jeon and Day (2016, 49 samples), both find a medium effect on reading comprehension and rate, around half a standard deviation, with longer programs doing better. Most of that research is on English learners in schools, so the exact size for an adult reading Slovak alone is a guess. The direction is not.

### 4. Input alone leaves accuracy gaps. This is the finding that matters most for the first learner.

Merrill Swain studied Canadian children who had spent years in French immersion, which is comprehensible input at scale. Their comprehension was near native. Their grammar in speech and writing was not: they understood everything and still got genders, agreement and verb forms wrong (Swain 1985). Her output hypothesis says that producing the language forces you to notice the gap between what you meant and what you can say, and that this noticing is where accuracy comes from. Lichtman and VanPatten still rate output as helping "sometimes, under some conditions", but the immersion result has been replicated enough that no serious program skips production.

The first learner's placement showed the same profile: reading around B1, case endings chosen by pattern memory rather than rule. A learner who already understands more than they can produce is the worst candidate for a method that postpones production.

### 5. Explicit teaching measurably helps, with one honest caveat.

Norris and Ortega's meta-analysis (2000, 49 studies) found explicit instruction (being told the rule, or having attention drawn to the form) roughly twice as effective as implicit exposure, with effect sizes near 1.1 against 0.5. Spada and Tomita (2010) and Goo and colleagues' replication (2015) found the same ordering. The caveat: most of those studies measured with tests that favor explicit knowledge, and Lichtman and VanPatten argue explicit knowledge does not turn into the implicit system that runs real-time speech. Skill-acquisition researchers (DeKeyser) answer that enough practice automatises it. For an adult drilling case endings, the practical reading is: a short rule plus lots of practice in real sentences gets you accurate production faster; input builds the system underneath.

### 6. Spaced retrieval is the best-supported technique in the whole field.

Karpicke and Roediger (2008) showed that recalling beats re-reading for retention. Kim and Webb's meta-analysis of spaced vocabulary practice (2022, 48 experiments) found spaced practice at an effect size of about 1.5 against 1.0 for the same time massed. The review deck, the retry-after-a-hint and the missed-item-comes-back rule in the lessons are on firm ground.

### 7. Nation's four strands is the balance the research points to.

Paul Nation's answer to the input-versus-practice argument is to stop arguing and split the time: about a quarter each on meaning-focused input (listening and reading for the message), meaning-focused output (speaking and writing for the message), language-focused learning (rules, vocabulary, pronunciation, feedback) and fluency development (going faster with what you already know). He adds a hard rule: no more than a quarter on deliberate language study.

### 8. The popular CI products: strong on volume, thin on evidence.

TPRS has a handful of small classroom studies, mostly positive for vocabulary and motivation (Lichtman's 2018 review). Dreaming Spanish and the Automatic Language Growth method behind it have thousands of testimonials and no controlled study. The claim that speaking early does lasting damage has no experimental support; Krashen himself only says output is not the cause of acquisition, not that it harms. Their real contribution is practical: a huge library of graded input, a visible hours count, and a culture of doing a lot of it.

## Where Lanna stands

Every activity in the app sorted into Nation's strands, with the minutes a full day's plan gives each one. The minutes are the plan's own estimates; the share is what matters.

"Now" counts the Listen step (4 min), the two input reps that open every new unit's lesson, and Read. Deliberate study still runs over Nation's quarter; the next moves are a real fluency strand and more output, not less grammar. The app now draws these bars itself, from the week's real ledger, under the daily plan.

### What the app already got right

- **Sentences, not words.** Every rep is a real sentence from slovak.wiki or Tatoeba with a translation and audio. That is the "communicatively embedded" condition, even inside a drill.
- **The 98% rule** in Story and now Read, measured against the learner's own known words rather than assumed.
- **Hint, retry, come back later** in lessons: retrieval with feedback, which is where accuracy comes from.
- **Spaced review** with FSRS, and missed words flowing into it from lessons.
- **Placement by evidence**, so the input level is measured, not guessed.

### Where it did not follow the evidence

- **No input strand.** On a Talk day the plan contained no listening or reading for meaning at all.
- **Test before meet.** A new unit's first rep asked the learner to choose the right form before they had seen it used once. Comprehension-precedes-production says the first contact should be an encounter, not an exam.
- **Nothing for listening.** Audio existed only as a prop for typing dictation. Listening for the message, which is most of real-world Slovak, had no task.
- **No fluency strand.** Blitz is speed on forms; there was no "read or hear something easy, fast".
- **No balance anywhere on screen.** The learner could not tell that three quarters of their minutes were going to one strand.

## What changed in the app

1. **A Listen step in the daily plan** (`/listen`), right after Review and before the lesson. Ten real sentences chosen from the dictionary against the same known-words estimate Read uses: at most one new word a sentence, and the ten together at least 93% known words. Each one is played first with the text hidden; the learner picks what it meant from three English meanings, then sees the text with the stretch word marked and glossed. No typing, no grammar grading. Stretch words go into the review deck. Audio comes from the cached library, so a sentence is paid for once.
2. **Input before output in every new unit's lesson.** The first two reps of a unit's first lesson are now "What does it mean?" reps: the whole sentence, spoken, with the target form highlighted and three English meanings to pick from. The choose and type reps follow. Once a unit is practiced, the lesson is production almost throughout, as before.
3. **This week's balance on Today.** Under the plan, four bars show the week's estimated minutes by strand against the quarter-each target, with the strand that is furthest behind named in one line. Minutes are the plan's estimates per activity, labeled as such. (Since 2026-10-29 a task with a timer counts the minutes measured while the learner was active, and the card says how many are still estimated; see P9.)
4. **Story counts as input, Talk as output** in that balance, and the plan's copy says so.
5. **A short day stays balanced.** When the planned minutes cannot hold everything, the plan now keeps one task per strand (Listen, then Speak) before a second drill, instead of cutting from the bottom of the list.

Not changed: the lesson patterns for practiced units, the review deck, Talk, Read, the tutor prompts. Open: a fluency strand (easy re-reading and re-listening at speed) and a second output task; both would move the balance further toward the target.

Where it lives in the code: `src/core/listen.ts`, `src/server/listen.ts`, `app/listen/`, `components/listen/listen-player.tsx` (the Listen task); `LESSON_PATTERN` and the `meaning` rep kind in `src/core/lesson.ts`; `BUDGET_ORDER` in `src/core/curriculum.ts`; `src/core/strands.ts` and `components/strand-balance.tsx` (the balance).
