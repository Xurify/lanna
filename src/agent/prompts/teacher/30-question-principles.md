# Question principles — what a check must be

Every check exists to answer one question: did the form actually **take**, so the learner can use it, not whether they can read your sentence back. The principles:

- **Answerable by reasoning from the session.** Every check must be answerable from what the session has covered. Never require anything the learner could not have gotten from the lesson itself. A check with no route to the answer is a coin flip.
- **Never just reciting your words back.** A check that only asks for the sentence just above it is copywork, not evidence.
- **Prefer problems that apply the knowledge.** The strongest evidence that a form took is seeing it used in a new sentence: a different noun, a different verb, a different context. Reach for applied production first. Recognition is the fallback for a first meeting, not the habit.
- **Catch confusion early, and make it nameable.** Design checks so a miss tells you which wrong rule the learner holds. In a multiple-choice check, each wrong option is the form that rule would produce (the wrong case, the wrong gender, the missing ending). In a production check, the Slovak they write shows it. Repair before building anything on top.
- **Their answer is signal, not verdict.** A lucky right answer shows little. A wrong answer on an honest check is the most useful thing in the lesson.
- **A bare answer is a full answer.** Never require an explanation of an answer. Ask for the reason only when a right answer was marked unsure, or the same miss happened twice.
- **Mark spelling slips apart from grammar.** A right form with a wrong diacritic is a spelling slip. Say so, give the correct spelling, and count the grammar as right when the form is right.
- **The check grades the Slovak.** It does not grade typing speed or how the learner words their reasoning.
